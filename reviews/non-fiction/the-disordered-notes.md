### Chapter 1

Early understanding of the brain was quite limited but we discovered astonishing facts about **how** our brains work via the strange cases doctors faced. For instance, a man suffering from syphillis approached doctor Broca in the 1860s with a strange problem in which we could understand everything but could not speak anything. His vocal chords were fine as demonstrated by his ability to hum but he couldn't actually say sentences. Broca found damage in the same front left region of the brain in more patients with similar problems and concluded that the ability to speak language, not comprehend it, was located in the front left hemisphere of the brain in a regiow now named Broca's area. Doctor Wernicke, a decade later, found that the comprehension of language was in the back of the left hemisphere after encountering many patients with comprehension problems and damage to that particular part of the brain. Given these two facts, Wernicke also predicted there would be problems for certain people with damage to the Arcuate Fasciculus which connects the two regions; he was right. 

Kandel goes over how the neuron and its functioning was discovered by Ramon y Cajal from Spain. Dendrites receive action potentials from other neurons via their synapses and the cell body determines whether or not to initiate an action potential (it's either a 1 or 0), and that gets sent to the axon and then to the synapses where it would send that action potential to other dendrites. Animal models, where animals with similar brain structures OR made to have similar genes as us, are used to understand the pathology as well as potential treatments in neuroscience. We also learn how fMRIs and PET scans can be used to image the brain while it is functioning through its blood flows or with more detail in PET scans. Kandel also goes over genetics and how different and complex it can get with brain disorders as some genetic mutations, like Huntingtons, are single gene while others can involve hundreds or thousands of genes and where the environment can play a large factor. This chapter lays down the foundations for the rest of the book.

### Chapter 2: Autism

Autism is a disorder with a broad spectrum of severity and symptoms and begins before toddlers turn 3. It typically involves and inability to behave socially in a typical fashion with other humans. While most toddlers, at the age of 3, gain what is called a "theory of mind" where around 3 or 4 most children can understand that someone else may not be thinking the same thing they are thinking; it is your ability to attribte different mental states to others and predict their behavior. While imaging the brains of children with autism, scientists have found they use the "mechanical" motion detecting part of their brains strictly while "normal" children use both the mechanical detection as well as "biological" motion detection. Autism has been shown to be roughly 90% correlated to genes which means there is very little environmental in nature that could cause autism; many mothers were blamed for autism while identical twin correlation showed that autism is almost entirely due to genetics. Autism was diagnosed and understood as disease only since the 1940s. Researchers have found that an extra copy of a segment on Chromosome 7 leads to a dramatically higher chance of autism while a deletion of that same segment leads to the opposite disorders: Williams syndrome. Children with autistm are often excellent drawers whereas children with Williams symdrome are sociable and have trouble with drawing but are excellent at music. 

Another factor that might underpin many of these austim spectrum disorders is de novo mutations. While women form eggs at birth, meaning the genes of those eggs are exactly (with exceptions due to environmental genetic mutations), the same as birth, men form sperm on a daily basis. This means that as they get older, they form more and more **de novo** mutations in their sperm. There is almost a direct correlation with the age of men and the rise in de novo mutations and odds that their offspring have autism or other mental disorders. By the time men are 40, they have almost 100 de novo mutations.

### Chapter 3: Depression and Bilpolar Disorder

One of the early pioneers of mental disorders divided mental disorders into two distinct categories: disorders of thought and disorders of mood. Depression, a disorder of mood, typically happens in late teenage years or early twenties. It is treatable and the underlyng neural circuitry behind it can in fact be reversible (for most cases). Schizophrenia, on the other hand, typically starts in young adulthood and the patients will go into decline from there on out with no fundamental reversal possible. Bipolar disorder will start around late adolescence. 

Depression can be triggered by stress and ironically actually triggers stress. Depression activates the prolonged release of cortisol which can damage and destroy synaptic connections in the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex. The specific region of the brain concerned with emotion is overactive relative to normal people in depressive episodes in patients with depression and those with bipolar disorder. 

Iprionazid, a drug used for Tuberculosis patients, was found to reduce the lethargy and increase the moods of depressed patients as well. Monoamine Oxidase breaks down and removes seratonin and noradrenaline. Iprionazid worked by blocking Monoamine Oxidase from nerve terminal uptake. This left more seratonin in the brain and might have led to the happier moods of patients taking it. While this helps some patients, many patients do not in fact get better with drugs that flood the brain with seratonin. Depression is also caused by stress which is not related to seratonin and still results in the damage to synapses so no drug is an actual cure to depression thus far. 

Aaron Beck, a psychoanalyst, discovered that many depressed patients tend to focus or solely remember negative events and aspects of themselves. This led him to experiment with and prove that reinforcing positive thoughts, memories, and ideas of patients to them was as good as and in many cases better than antidepressants. His therapy, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, is a major advance in treating depression albeit not for the most severe depression cases. It has been found that for **severe** cases of depression, a combination of cognitive behavioral therapy and drugs works much better than drugs alone.
