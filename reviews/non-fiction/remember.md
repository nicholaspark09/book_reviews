# Book Review: Remember

## Book Information
- **Title**: Remember
- **Author**: Lisa Genova
- **Year Published**: 2021
- **Publisher**: Random House
- **Genre/Category**: Non-Fiction / Science & Psychology    
- **Length**: 272 pages

## Overview
*Brief introduction to the book, author, and your overall assessment (3-4 sentences)*
Lisa Genova is a neuroscientist delving into the memory aspect of the human brain in multiple books as evidenced
by her references to a seemingly more popular "Still Alice" (It is referenced enough in the book to make it clear
she is both proud and well known enough that most readers probably start with that book). Remember is a short walk
through human memory with both well known studies and personal stories of acquaintances and family members. Much of
the text is easy to read, fast, but does little to distinguish this book from others on memory as the cases she 
walks through are both well known and not unique. There is very little distinguishing *Remember* from *Why We Remember*.
I enjoyed the book as it was a nice refresher on the broad outlines of memory, but there was very little by the way
of revelatory or substantive information. 

## Summary
*Concise overview of the book's main content and structure (1-2 paragraphs)*
Remember goes through the broad outlines of memory, the various types and stages of memory, the strengths of human
memory and eventually the ways in which it can fail or degrade by diverse means. Lisa Genova is able to go over memory
is three broad stages: How we remember, Why we forget, and Improve or Impair. In How We Remember, we learn as an audience
that memory works by encoding the information, consolidating the information, storing the information, and eventually
retrieving the information. Lisa is also able to demonstrate through case studies (such as Patient HM) how short term 
memory gets stored to long term memory via the hippocampus. We discover:
- working memory: short term memory where we tend to remember at most 7 digits for a period of between 15-30 seconds
- muscle memory: information stored in our basal ganglia that helps us to physically do things without having to "think" 
much about doing them
- semantic memory: knowledge and hard facts
- episodic memory: information tied to context with a "when" and "where"
- prospective memory: actions you need to perform in the future

As highlighted and indicated by the author, memory only becomes stored if it moves from the working memory to the hippocampus
which connects it to a time and place. Memory is then transferred to long term storage which seems to be a vast network across the brain that only works if it's activated and in that same connected activation. It isn't stored permanently and needs to be activated over time to "remember" the connections between neurons and strengthen them. 

Lisa then highlights the ability and importance of forgetting as the second section of the book. One important detail mentioned
repeatedly in this book is that in order to actually remember something, we need to pay "attention" to it. The prefrontal cortex,
the "CEO" of our brain, needs to be used to direct our attention to something in order to remember it in the first place.
Much information that happens around us is ignored intentionally so as to allow us to focus on relevant details. We cannot remember
what we do not "choose" to remember through directed attention; modern phones and social media are directly correlated with reduced abilities to focus with the result being smaller prefrontal cortices. Our brains often "forget" intentionally in order
to allow us to retrieve relevant information without having to go through trillions of irrelevant details. This was demonstrated
by the story of Shereshevky, a Soviet reporter, that remember every detail of his life which made it difficult for him to forget
details he did not want to remember. A highlighted portion of this section for me was the point that many memories use schemas 
in order to be more efficient but that means we don't actually remember the true specifics. We also tend to "overwrite" or edit
our memories of events when "remembering" incidents and that those memories, via suggestions or mistakes, can then be imprinted
back onto our network of neurons causing us to forever "remember" those events incorrectly.

In the final portion of Remember, the author goes over the degradation or decline of brains that come with aging and some ways
they can be mitigated. While our semantic repositories grow larger over time, our abilities to retrieve that information and 
act on it slows down or degrades. As we age, the amyloid clumps and tau tangles increase inhibiting our brains neural connections
in ways that reduce our ability to retrieve the information they hold. It is important to eat heart healthy diets, exercise,
and sleep regularly to reduce toxins in our brains and allow healthy blood flow. This can reduce the speed or damage that comes 
with time but it cannot prevent it or stop it. Eventually, over 1/10 individuals will get alzheimer's over the age of 65 and by 85 that jumps to 1/3. Alzheimer's starts in the entorhinal cortex (not from this book but from Our Brains, Our Selves), which is where
short term memory moves to the hippocampus. That means patients first start having trouble with short term memory and as it gets to the hippocampus they can't or have trouble forming new long term memories. Then as it spreads to the parietal lobes, they have difficulty with spatial awareness or navigation, and when it gets to the rest of the brain from there they lose their long term memories. When it gets to the prefrontal cortex they have trouble with attention, logic, decision making, and planning. The amygdala also may break down causing a lot of emotional control and when it gets to the basal ganglia, they forget how to do physical things like swallowing food or using the restroom.



## Writing Style and Accessibility
*Assessment of the author's writing quality, clarity, and who can easily engage with this book*
Genova did a wonderful job of adding an appendix that acts as a review for the book helping us remember the key sections and details. She demonstrated the "spacing effect" and "retrieval" that is necessary to form a memory so that we as readers can more easily carry her stressed details with us. 

## Strengths
*What the book does particularly well*
- Convey complex neurological details through case studies and well defined sections
- The author practices good memory habits in the book itself leaving the reader with a better ability to recall and retain its contents
- The author **does not mislead** readers on the capabilities of memory nor its eventual fate. Many authors exaggerate or imply an ability to fend off or cure memory deficits that come with ageing, but Lisa Genova sticks to biological truths thus far known.

## Weaknesses
*Areas where the book falls short or could be improved*
- Many of these facts or case studies are actually well known and told over and over again in various books on human brains so many times that it almost feels like I've read this book before.
- Lisa Genova does not include any studies that she has performed or much in the way of novel information.
- There is not much of a technical "deep dive" and it is very much lacking in actual processes so disappointing for someone looking for that information.

## Key Takeaways
*The most valuable insights I gained*
- I learned that naps do in fact help us to remember things by moving information from our hippocampus into long term storage. Doing that allows the hippocampus to be "freer" for adding context to and moving information from our short term memory into long term memory.
- Almost half of us will have Alzheimer's or some sort of memory deficit by the time we're "old."
- Context is very important in retrieving information (and "edges") so that in order to retrieve information in a faster fashion, we should try to place ourselves in the same context which includes smell, coffee, music, location, etc.

## Impact and Application
*How this book affected my thinking and what I've implemented from it*
I'm not as bothered by tip of the tongue moments as the average person gets two+- per day. One large takeway from this book is that I should be keeping a journal to help me remember things as my mind will warp information as time goes by. My memories can't be trusted and I can and should use some technology to keep details, but I need to avoid phones/notifications as I will not be able to encode information with them around.

## Comparison
*How this book compares to similar works in the field*
This book does not provide any novel insight into memory that can distinguish it from other books on the same topic. I found "Our Brains, Our Selves" to be much better at both technical and clinical aspects. However, this book was much better at applying memory techniques in such a way as to help me actually remember its contents clearly. Very clever.

## Who Should Read This
*Specific recommendations for who would benefit most*
Anyone who wants to start learning about how our brains work in memory.

## Who Should Skip This
*Types of readers who might want to look elsewhere*
People with backgrounds in biology or neuroscience that are looking for deep dives on the brain or memory mechanisms.

## Final Thoughts
*Closing perspective on the book's value and lasting impression*
I wish I had read this book before some other books on memory and the human brain. Had I read it before, "Our Brains, Our Selves", I think would have enjoyed that book even more as "Remember" is a wonderful broad overview of memory and how our brains preserve memory. Lisa Genova goes over the types of memory we have, how we actually store and use those memories, and how we can help preserve them in meaningful ways. She offers some practical ways of preventing mistakes in memory, like checklists and memory palaces, and some new insights on memory such as context relevance. This was a very fast and fun read which I would recommend to everyone apart from neuroscientists or people already familiar with memory.
